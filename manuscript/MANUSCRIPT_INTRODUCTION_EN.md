**The Inflammation Niche**

*Review manuscript — Introduction draft (citations numbered in order of first appearance)*

# Introduction

Tissue injury and inflammation are inseparable. Infiltrating inflammatory cells multitask at the damaged site, mediating wound debridement while producing chemokines, metabolites and growth factors that orchestrate repair and regeneration [1]. The outcome of this metabolically demanding program is finely balanced: when the inflammatory response resolves appropriately and the tissue retains sufficient regenerative capacity, architecture and function are restored; when the response becomes dysregulated, healing degenerates into a chronic wound or progressive fibrosis that increasingly impairs organ function [1]. The pancreas traverses both trajectories. In acute pancreatitis, environmental and genetic stressors first injure the organelles of the acinar cell—the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria and the endolysosomal-autophagy system—precipitating the inappropriate intracellular activation of trypsinogen and inflammatory pathways, while damage-associated molecular patterns propagate local inflammation into a systemic response that can culminate in extrapancreatic organ failure [2]. When injury persists or recurs, inflammation becomes chronic: pancreatic stellate cells and infiltrating immune cells undergo metabolic reprogramming—dominated by enhanced glycolysis and lipid metabolism—that fuels excessive extracellular matrix deposition and irreversible fibrosis [3]. Step by step, therefore, the inflamed pancreas acquires the defining properties of a pathological niche: sustained inflammatory signalling, a perpetually remodelling stroma and a reshaped metabolic landscape.

That chronic inflammation predisposes to cancer is among the oldest clinical observations in oncology, and the pancreas has supplied its most rigorous experimental demonstration. Adult mice expressing an endogenous K-RAS oncogene in acinar cells are surprisingly refractory to transformation, yet a single course of mild chronic pancreatitis unlocks the entire spectrum of pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasias (PanINs) and invasive ductal adenocarcinoma, establishing that pancreatic tumorigenesis requires the cooperation of genetic lesions with non-genetic tissue damage [4]. How, mechanistically, does the inflammatory niche confer this competence? Recent studies converge on epigenetic memory and plasticity as the operative currency. Recurrent pancreatitis imprints a lasting "epithelial memory" of inflammation that limits damage upon re-injury but simultaneously predisposes to tumorigenesis, because inflammatory episodes select for epithelial clones whose carcinogenic alterations confer protection from inflammatory damage [5]. Even a transient, KRAS-independent acinar-to-ductal metaplasia leaves behind a DNA methylation memory that persists after morphological recovery and primes KRAS-downstream PI3K and Rho GTPase signalling [6]. Conversely, when oncogenic KRAS and tissue damage coincide, the epithelium switches within 48 hours into a unique "acinar-to-neoplasia" chromatin state—distinct from normal regeneration and selected for throughout malignant evolution; among the factors most rapidly induced by injury, the alarmin interleukin-33 suffices to recapitulate this cooperation with mutant KRAS in unleashing the epigenetic remodelling program of early neoplasia [7]. At single-cell resolution, such gene–environment interactions generate remarkable heterogeneity: morphologically indistinguishable premalignant cells occupy distinct epigenetically primed states, specified by chromatin remodelling at cell–cell communication loci and consolidated by reciprocal feedback between epithelial and immune populations [8]. Collectively, these findings recast the inflammatory niche as an instructive niche: it records inflammatory experience, translates it into gene-regulatory commitment, and exerts selective pressure that favours the fittest mutants.

Crucially, construction of the niche is bidirectional—transformed cells rapidly become architects of their own environment. Oncogenic KRAS engages stromal fibroblasts and, in return, receives reciprocal signals transmitted through an IGF1R/AXL–AKT axis that roughly doubles the number of regulated signalling nodes relative to cell-autonomous KRAS alone and rewrites tumour-cell proliferation, apoptosis and mitochondrial capacity [9]. Tumour-suppressor mutations contribute in kind. Mutant p53-expressing cancer cells release exosomes whose podocalyxin cargo instructs normal fibroblasts to deposit a pro-invasive extracellular matrix and remotely conditions distant organs such as the lung into pre-metastatic terrain [10]. In gastric cancer, mutant TP53 even rewrites cytokine dependence during progression—switching from interleukin-11 to interleukin-6 within the gp130 family—so that tumour fitness comes to hinge on a druggable IL6–STAT3 circuit [11]. Nor are such inflammatory-niche signals confined to a single organ: hepatocellular senescence broadcasts via TGFβ to instate senescence and dysfunction in remote, otherwise healthy organs [12]. The inflammation niche is therefore best understood not as static soil but as a dynamic, self-reinforcing circuit—negotiated among mutant epithelium, stromal cells and immune cells, and capable of extending its reach across the organism.

This circuit view carries a therapeutic corollary: striking the niche's central oncogenic node should rewire the entire ecosystem. Inhibiting KRASG12D with MRTX1133 regresses both early PanIN lesions and established tumours while simultaneously remodelling the microenvironment—restoring intratumoral CD8+ effector T cells, restraining myeloid infiltration, reprogramming cancer-associated fibroblasts and inducing FAS on tumour cells to permit T-cell-mediated killing—and synergizes with immune checkpoint blockade to eradicate disease [13]. Equally remarkable is the selectivity of this pharmacology: the RAS-GTP multi-selective inhibitor RMC-7977 evokes waves of apoptosis and sustained proliferative arrest in pancreatic tumours, whereas normal tissues undergo only a transient and reversible decline in proliferation [14]. Building on these observations, it has been argued that RAS inhibitors should be developed deliberately as immunotherapies: their full antitumour activity depends on T cells and cross-presenting dendritic cells, and their combination with checkpoint blockade, vaccines, bispecific antibodies and cell therapy may finally render pancreatic cancer responsive to immunotherapy, including in the adjuvant, neoadjuvant and interception settings [15]. Targeting the inflammation niche, in other words, is no longer only a stromal strategy; it is an oncological one.

In this Review, we introduce the concept of the inflammation niche to integrate these threads—injury-induced assembly, epigenetic memory, reciprocal epithelial–stromal–immune crosstalk and systemic extension—into a unified framework of how inflammation shapes cancer initiation, progression and response to therapy. Taking the pancreas as the central paradigm, and drawing on evidence from the stomach, liver and metastatic organs, we trace the life cycle of the inflammation niche from acute injury to invasive cancer, examine the cellular and molecular circuitry that sustains it, and discuss how niche-directed interventions, exemplified by the new generation of RAS inhibitors, are beginning to convert this once-permissive soil into therapeutic terrain.
